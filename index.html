Does a Simple Trend-Following Strategy Beat Buy-and-Hold?¶

A moving-average crossover backtest on SPY, with transaction costs and an honest out-of-sample test¶

Author: Matin Nikoomanzar

Question. The 50/200-day moving-average crossover (the "golden cross") is one of the oldest trend-following rules in finance: hold the S&P 500 when its 50-day average price is above its 200-day average, sit in cash otherwise. Does this rule actually beat simply buying and holding the index — after transaction costs, on data it was not tuned on?

Method.

  1. Download ~25 years of dividend-adjusted SPY prices (yfinance).
  2. Split the data: 2000–2017 is in-sample (used to pick the MA windows), 2018–present is out-of-sample (touched exactly once, at the end).
  3. Grid-search MA window pairs on the in-sample window only.
  4. Freeze the winning parameters and evaluate them out-of-sample with 5 bps per-side transaction costs.
  5. Compare CAGR, volatility, Sharpe ratio, and maximum drawdown against buy-and-hold.

Why the split matters. If I picked the parameters that look best on all the data and then reported that same data as "performance", the result would be overfit by construction. The out-of-sample window is the only honest scorecard.

2. The strategy, and the two classic backtesting traps¶

Rule. Long SPY when the fast moving average is above the slow one; in cash otherwise.

Trap 1 — lookahead bias. The moving averages use today's close, so the earliest the signal can be traded is tomorrow. backtest_core.ma_crossover_positions shifts the position by one day to enforce this. Skipping that shift inflates results with information no trader could have had.

Trap 2 — free trading. Every position change here pays 5 bps (0.05%). A round trip costs 10 bps. Crossover strategies trade rarely, so the drag is modest — but it must be in the numbers, not in a footnote.

6. Conclusions — written after looking at the numbers above¶

(Re-run the notebook, then make sure each claim below matches your actual output table. Edit if your numbers tell a different story.)

  1. Risk reduction is the real story. Trend-following rules like this one historically earn their keep not by out-returning the index but by cutting volatility and maximum drawdown — the strategy sidesteps part of the worst bear markets by going to cash. Check whether the out-of-sample max drawdown is meaningfully shallower than buy-and-hold's.

  2. Raw return usually loses. In strong bull markets the rule lags, because every whipsaw (a crossover that reverses quickly) pays costs and misses rebound days. If the out-of-sample CAGR trails buy-and-hold, that is the expected, honest result — not a failure of the analysis.

  3. The in-sample edge shrinks out-of-sample. Some of what the grid search "found" was noise. The gap between the two columns in Section 5 is a direct measurement of that overfitting.

Limitations I would address next¶

  • Risk-free rate = 0 in the Sharpe ratio; with cash earning ~4-5%, time spent out of the market should be credited, which would help the strategy.
  • Single asset, single regime. One index, one 25-year window. A serious test would span multiple assets and decades.
  • No execution realism. Fills at the close, fixed 5 bps cost, no taxes — taxes alone could erase the edge for a taxable investor.

What I learned¶

Building this taught me more about why most backtests are wrong than about this particular strategy: lookahead bias, cost drag, and in-sample selection each silently flatter results, and each had to be explicitly engineered away.
